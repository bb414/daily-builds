How it works and what it leaves out

Reported against real

Company carbon reports usually multiply electricity use by the grid's average intensity (the location-based method). Moving a job into an hour with a lower average makes the reported footprint fall. The atmosphere only notices the plant that actually ramps up to serve the extra load: the marginal plant. When gas is on the margin almost all the time, a cleaner-looking average hour is still a gas hour, so the real saving is small. Real savings come from using power that would otherwise be thrown away (curtailed wind and solar), or from avoiding hours when a dirtier plant is on the margin. Because the marginal plant is usually dirtier than the average, the real saving can also be larger than the report shows.

The grid

Each hour of a 52-week year, synthetic demand, wind and solar are dispatched in merit order: nuclear and renewables first, a minimum amount of thermal plant kept running for stability, then each thermal fleet from its most to its least efficient unit. If renewables exceed what is needed, the surplus is curtailed and the marginal intensity is zero. Forecasts are the true values plus errors that grow with lead time. All numbers are invented and loosely shaped, not calibrated to any real country.

The schedulers

    Limits

    • Short-run marginal emissions only. Longer-run effects, such as flexible demand making it easier to build more renewables, are left out; some practitioners argue those effects favour average signals.
    • The jobs are treated as small next to the grid, so they never change which plant runs.
    • No interconnectors, storage, network constraints, or switching between coal and gas as carbon prices change.
    • Simplified and illustrative: a teaching model, not a carbon accounting tool.
