    How it works

    Each simulated challenge draws a fresh field. Your rivals' true skill (the Sharpe ratio their strategy would earn over a long run) is spread around zero, because most student strategies have no lasting edge. A share of them are gamblers who run several times the usual risk but are no more skilled than anyone else. Every team's daily return is its skill times its risk plus luck, and part of the luck is a market move that everyone shares.

    Your team is then run at your chosen risk and at twenty levels from 5% to 100% volatility on exactly the same luck, so the risk curve shows the effect of risk alone. Teams are ranked by the rule you choose: highest return; highest Sharpe ratio, which divides return by volatility so leverage cannot help; highest Sortino ratio, which divides return by the size of the losing days alone, so upside swings are not penalised; best return over worst drawdown, which divides return by the largest fall from a peak; or highest return with any team whose account falls further than the limit below its peak knocked out. A team that loses everything is out under any rule.

    Going for broke. Your team, and a share of the steady rivals, can switch to a much higher risk if they are outside the top three at a checkpoint. Rivals judge their position against the other rivals, so their choices do not depend on yours and the risk curve still compares like with like. Studies of fund managers find the same behaviour: managers who are behind part-way through the year tend to raise their risk (Brown, Harlow and Starks, 1996; Chevalier and Ellison, 1997).

    Capping and costing risk. With a volatility cap, no team may run more than the cap, whatever it wants: gamblers, teams going for broke and your team included. The cost of risk is a return lost each year in proportion to volatility, so it costs a gambler several times what it costs a steady team. Over a few weeks it is small next to luck, which is why a cap changes the leaderboard far more than a cost.

    Checking a real leaderboard. Each team's result is turned into a luck-adjusted score: its log return divided by its volatility over the challenge, or its Sharpe ratio scaled to the challenge's length. If no team had any skill, those scores would spread out only as much as luck allows, and how much that is depends on how much teams ride the same market. More spread than that is a sign of skill: a chi-square test gives how often luck alone would produce it, and the excess gives an estimate of how much true skill varies across the field. Each team's estimated edge is its result pulled back towards the field's average by the share of the spread that is luck (a standard allowance for luck known as shrinkage). How often a field with no skill produces a best result as strong as the table's comes from 10,000 simulated luck-only fields. Tested on simulated challenges with no skill anywhere, it finds more spread than luck allows (at the 5% level) in about 5% of them, as a fair test should.

    Two thousand challenges are simulated each time. "New luck" draws a different two thousand, which shows how much the numbers move by chance: usually by less than a point.

    What it means

    • For teams: in a short challenge ranked on return, the leaderboard mostly measures risk and luck. If the goal is to win, more risk is usually the answer; if the goal is to learn, judge yourselves on risk-adjusted results, not the rank.
    • For organisers: ranking on Sharpe ratio removes the reward for leverage, a drawdown limit caps it, and a longer challenge gives skill more time to show. None of them makes a few weeks long enough to pick out the best team reliably.

    Limits

    • Everything is simulated. Real returns have more structure than a normal distribution with an optional fat tail, and real strategies are not equally good every day.
    • Apart from one go-for-broke checkpoint, teams keep the same risk all challenge. Real teams adjust all the time.
    • The shared market move is a single factor. Real teams overlap in more complicated ways.
    • The leaderboard check treats each team's volatility as its true risk and assumes daily returns behind any Sharpe ratio. If teams' luck is less shared than the correlation you set, it finds skill too readily, so when unsure a lower correlation is the cautious choice.
