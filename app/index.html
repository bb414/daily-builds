Why this matters

The usual answer to "can we trust this AI?" is "a person will check it". Human oversight is written into the EU AI Act, whose Article 14 asks that people overseeing high-risk systems stay aware of the tendency to over-rely on the output, which it calls automation bias. But a reviewer only adds value if they know when to trust the AI. A reviewer who agrees with everything adds nothing, and one who overrules the AI too often makes things worse.

That is one quiet reason AI pilots stall. The pilot measures the AI on its own, it looks good, and then the deployed system (a person plus the AI) does no better than before, so the return never shows up.

The key idea. When a person and an AI disagree, exactly one of them is right. Any reliance habit that treats every disagreement the same way (always defer, never defer, defer 70% of the time) lands somewhere between the two solo accuracies. Beating both needs a signal that tells you which disagreements to give to the AI, and the only places that signal can come from are confidence: the AI's and your own.

The exercise

The "Try it yourself" tab uses a two-step design common in research on AI advice: you decide first, then see the advice, then decide again. That separates your own judgement from the AI's influence. The payments are generated from six cues with fixed odds of fraud, so an ideal reader of the cues would get about 85% right. The advisor's mistakes are chosen in advance, so it is right exactly as often as you set. You can put its mistakes anywhere, on the tricky payments (where the clues point the wrong way, so you are likely to be fooled too) or on the obvious ones (where you should catch them).

Two measures, from Schemmer and colleagues (2023), summarise how you used the advice:

  • Switching when it helped (relative AI reliance): of the payments where you were wrong and the AI was right, how often you switched to the AI.
  • Holding firm when it mattered (relative self-reliance): of the payments where you were right and the AI was wrong, how often you kept your answer.

Appropriate reliance means both are high. Over-reliance shows up as a low second number, under-reliance as a low first number.

The model

The "Explore the model" tab works out the same thing exactly, for any assumptions. It needs:

  • the person's and the AI's accuracy on their own;
  • error overlap: how far they tend to get the same cases wrong. Errors they share can never be fixed by any rule;
  • how much each side's confidence tells you about whether it is right (in signal detection terms, d', from 0 for "no information" to 4 for "almost perfect"), and whether either side is overconfident.

It then compares four rules for settling disagreements: deferring at a fixed rate, following the AI whenever it shows at least a set confidence, going with whoever is surer, and the best possible rule, which weighs both confidences exactly. Every result is calculated exactly, not simulated, and each rule's errors are split into the three kinds in the first chart.

The other views answer follow-up questions. The map shows, for every combination of the two accuracies, whether a rule lets the pair beat both. The levers chart nudges each assumption in turn to show what would help most: a more accurate model, more honest confidence, or a reviewer whose mistakes differ from the AI's. The last chart shows what happens as you raise the confidence bar for following the AI.

One result is worth knowing: if both sides' confidence is honest and their errors are unrelated, "go with whoever is surer" is the best possible rule. Overconfidence on either side, or errors that cluster on the same cases, is what breaks it.

Limits

  • The model is a simplification. Real confidence is coarser and people's habits drift as they learn whether the AI tends to be right.
  • A session of 20 payments is a small sample. Treat your own numbers as a conversation starter, not a diagnosis.
  • The payment cues and their odds are made up for teaching. They are not a real fraud model.
