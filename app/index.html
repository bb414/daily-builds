    Why this matters

    The usual answer to "can we trust this AI?" is "a person will check it". Human oversight is written into the EU AI Act, whose Article 14 asks that people overseeing high-risk systems stay aware of the tendency to over-rely on the output, which it calls automation bias. But a reviewer only adds value if they know when to trust the AI. A reviewer who agrees with everything adds nothing, and one who overrules the AI too often makes things worse.

    That is one quiet reason AI pilots stall. The pilot measures the AI on its own and looks good. The deployed system is a person plus the AI, it does no better than before, and the promised return never shows up. This model asks what has to be true for the pair to beat both of its members.

    The key idea. In a yes or no decision, when a person and an AI disagree, exactly one of them is right. Any habit that treats every disagreement the same way (always defer, never defer, defer 70% of the time) lands somewhere between the two solo accuracies. Beating both needs a signal that tells you which disagreements to give to the AI, and the only places that signal can come from are confidence: the AI's and the person's own.

    What you set

    • the person's and the AI's accuracy on their own;
    • error overlap: how far they tend to get the same cases wrong. Mistakes they share can never be fixed by any rule;
    • how much each side's confidence tells you about whether it is right (in signal detection terms, d', from 0 for "no information" to 4 for "almost perfect");
    • whether either side is overconfident (or underconfident), which shifts the confidence it shows without changing how informative it is.

    The four rules

    • Defer at a fixed rate: hand a set share of disagreements to the AI, whatever either side says. This is what rubber-stamping (100%) and ignoring the AI (0%) look like.
    • Follow the AI when it sounds sure: take its answer whenever its shown confidence clears a bar.
    • Go with whoever is surer: compare the two shown confidences.
    • Best possible rule: weigh both confidences perfectly, using what each one really says about being right. No rule that uses the same information can do better, so it is the ceiling.

    One result is worth knowing: if both sides' confidence is honest and their mistakes are unrelated, "go with whoever is surer" is the best possible rule. Overconfidence on either side, or mistakes that cluster on the same cases, is what breaks it.

    How it is worked out

    Each side has a hidden signal on every case that is higher, on average, when it is right; its shown confidence is a calibrated reading of that signal, shifted up if it is overconfident. Together with the two accuracies and the error overlap, this gives exact formulas for how often each rule follows the AI when the AI is right and when it is wrong. Nothing is simulated, so every chart updates instantly and the same inputs always give the same answer. The table under the first chart also shows each rule's rate of switching when it helped and holding firm when it mattered, the two measures of appropriate reliance from Schemmer and colleagues (2023).

    The charts answer follow-up questions. "Look closer at one rule" turns the rates into 100 typical cases. The map shows, for every combination of the two accuracies, whether a rule lets the pair beat both. The levers chart nudges each assumption in turn to show what would help most: a more accurate model, more informative confidence, or a reviewer whose mistakes differ from the AI's. The last chart shows what happens as the bar for following the AI rises.

    Limits

    • This is a model, not a measurement. Every number follows from the assumptions you set; to see how a real person relies on an AI, you need to test people.
    • Real confidence is coarser than a smooth signal, and people's habits drift as they learn whether the AI tends to be right. The model has no learning.
    • It covers yes or no decisions with one right answer. Tasks with many possible answers, or no clear ground truth, behave differently.
