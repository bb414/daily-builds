      How it works

      The statement

      Each person has habits: how often they use each invented shop, on which days, and roughly how much they spend. A seeded random generator turns those habits into a statement with realistic bank descriptions: card terminal prefixes, store numbers, town names, order references, direct debits and bank transfers with a reference. Every payment also carries the pot that person would choose, which is the truth the app is scored against.

      The app's model

      The app is a naive Bayes text classifier, the classic technique behind early spam filters and many transaction categorisers. It reads the payment channel (card terminal, other card payment, wallet, direct debit or transfer), a merchant key (the first two meaningful words), every word in the description, an amount band and weekday or weekend. Before you touch it, it has learned from about 1,360 invented payments filed the way most people file them. Each payment you check is added to its training data, counted as many times as the weight setting says, so your own habits soon outweigh the crowd's.

      Why the app gets things wrong
      • It has never seen your local shops. A small café or pub is only recognisable from generic words such as CAFE or BAR, and some names contain none.
      • Your conventions are not the crowd's. Rent paid to a flatmate looks like a transfer, a meal deal looks like groceries and a late taxi looks like transport.
      • One merchant, several pots. The same supermarket can be the weekly shop or the pre-drinks shop. Only the amount and the day hint at which.
      How the strategies choose
      • Random spot checks: any unchecked payment.
      • Least confident first: the payment the model is least sure about.
      • Biggest amounts first: the largest unchecked payment.
      • Most common merchants first: one payment from the merchant that appears most often and has not been checked yet.
      • Most money at risk first: adds up amount × (1 − confidence) for each merchant and asks about the merchant with the most money that is probably misfiled.

      “Wrong pot” is half the sum of the gaps between what the app says and what was really spent in each pot, so it counts every misplaced pound once.

      Limits

      Everything is invented, and real apps use richer models, merchant databases and rules. The point is the shape of the problem: a generic model meets personal conventions, and the order in which an app asks for help decides how soon the budget can be trusted. This is an educational tool, not financial advice.

      Mislabelled runs entirely in your browser. Nothing is uploaded or stored. All people, shops and payments are invented.
