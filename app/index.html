How it works and what it leaves out

Reported against real

Company carbon reports usually multiply electricity use by the grid's average intensity in each hour (the location-based method). Moving a job into an hour with a lower average makes the reported footprint fall. But the atmosphere only notices the plant that actually ramps up to serve the extra load: the marginal plant. When gas is marginal almost all the time, a cleaner-looking average hour is still a gas hour, so the real saving is small. Real savings come from using power that would otherwise be thrown away (curtailed wind and solar), or from avoiding hours when a dirtier plant is on the margin.

The grid

Each hour of a 52-week year, synthetic demand, wind and solar are dispatched in merit order: nuclear and renewables first, a minimum amount of gas kept running for stability, then thermal plant from cleanest to dirtiest within each fleet. If renewables exceed what is needed, the surplus is curtailed and the marginal intensity is zero. All numbers are invented and loosely shaped, not calibrated to any real country.

    Limits

    • Short-run marginal emissions only: the model does not capture longer-run effects, such as flexible demand encouraging more renewables to be built.
    • The jobs are treated as small next to the grid, so they do not change prices or which plant runs.
    • No interconnectors, storage, network constraints or fuel switching with carbon prices.
    • Simplified and illustrative: this is a teaching model, not a carbon accounting tool.
