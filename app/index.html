Quiet cartel

Can self-learning pricing algorithms learn to keep prices high without ever being told to, and could a regulator tell from prices alone?

What this is

A laboratory for one of the stranger questions in competition policy: can pricing algorithms that only ever try to maximise their own profit learn to keep prices high together, with no agreement, no messages and no instruction to collude? And if they can, what should a regulator do about it, and could it even tell?

The market

Sellers offer similar products. Each day every shopper picks the product (or nothing) that gives them the best mix of quality, price and personal taste, which gives the standard logit demand curve. The competitive price is where no seller can gain by changing its price on its own for one day (the static Nash equilibrium). The monopoly price is what the sellers would charge if one owner set every price to maximise their joint profit.

The algorithms

Each learning seller runs Q-learning, a simple and widely taught reinforcement learning method. It keeps a table of how valuable each price is in each situation (yesterday’s prices), mostly charges the best price it knows, and sometimes tries a random one. Experimenting starts at 100% of days and fades over time. When no seller’s best price has changed in any situation for 100,000 rounds, training stops.

Measures

Profit gain
Where profits settle between competitive (0%) and monopoly (100%). Measured once the algorithms play the strategies they learned.
Shoppers’ loss
The same scale for consumer surplus: 0% is what shoppers get at competitive prices, 100% what they get at monopoly prices.
Punished
A one-day price cut that would pay on the day if nobody reacted is answered by a rival with a lower price than it would otherwise have charged, and the cut loses the cutter money once the later losses are counted.
Held up by a threat
Punished, and with a profit gain of at least 20%. This is the case file’s hidden truth for “coordinated”.
Best reply
The strategy that earns the most over time against the rival’s actual learned strategy, found exactly by value iteration. If every seller already plays its best reply, the outcome is an equilibrium.

Limits

  • All data is synthetic and the sellers are invented. Prices are in pounds only to make them readable.
  • Real pricing systems learn from far richer data, change prices at different times and often use other methods than tabular Q-learning. The results here show what is possible, not how common it is.
  • The legal tests in this app are simplified illustrations. Whether algorithmic tacit collusion is unlawful is an open question in UK and EU competition law. Nothing here is legal advice.
  • The market set-up follows a well-known academic design (Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò and Pastorello, American Economic Review, 2020), rebuilt from scratch here; the numbers are this app’s own.
