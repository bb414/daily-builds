Quiet cartel: companion report

Can self-learning pricing algorithms learn to keep prices high without being told to, which remedies stop them, does a pilot predict what happens when a rival adopts one too, how robust is that to the assumptions, and could a regulator tell from the prices? This report collects results from the Python companion to the Quiet cartel browser app.

All markets, sellers and sales here are synthetic, produced by simulated Q-learning sellers in a textbook logit demand model. The numbers are illustrative only: they are not evidence about any real business, and nothing here is legal advice.

Change is the mean difference from the paired baseline run, with a 90% bootstrap interval over the pairs. The p-value is a two-sided sign-flip permutation test (exact for 16 pairs or fewer), adjusted with Holm’s method across all the options shown. Shoppers’ loss is the share of the gap between competitive and monopoly consumer surplus that shoppers lose. Punished is the share of runs in which a one-day price cut is answered and loses money overall; settled is the share whose strategies stopped changing. Gain from best reply is, averaged over runs, how much more the learning seller with more to gain would earn over time, from where play settled, by switching to its exact best reply to the others’ learned strategies, from the model’s best-response check (two sellers only). Zero would mean the learned strategies form an equilibrium.

What each option means
Both learn and see all prices
The textbook case: two self-learning sellers who can see what each other charged yesterday.
Rivals’ prices banned as an input
Each algorithm may only use its own past price, not what rivals charged.
Rewarded on today’s profit only
The algorithm is told to maximise today’s profit, with no weight on the future.
A third seller enters
Another self-learning seller joins the market.
Rival keeps a fixed competitive price
One seller ignores the algorithm and always charges the competitive price.
Must keep testing prices (5% of days)
A rule that algorithms keep experimenting, so no pattern is ever fully settled.
No memory at all
Neither algorithm remembers any past price, so neither can punish.
Rival matches the cheapest price
One seller uses a simple price-match rule instead of learning.
Both buy the same pricing tool
One vendor’s algorithm learns from both sellers’ sales at once (hub and spoke).
Busy and quiet days
Demand swings from day to day, so profits are noisier to learn from.

Does the pilot predict the result at scale?

Across all 60 runs, A’s uplift in the pilot against A’s uplift after B adopts: Pearson r 0.07, Spearman rho 0.12. Within each rule:

  • B keeps the competitive price (20 runs): n/a (A’s uplift did not vary)
  • B matches the cheapest price (20 runs): Pearson r 0.06, Spearman rho −0.04
  • B undercuts by one step (20 runs): n/a (A’s uplift did not vary)

Pearson’s r measures how close the runs lie to a straight line; Spearman’s rho is the same on ranks, so it asks only whether a better pilot goes with a better result at scale. Near +1: the pilot predicts the scaled result; near 0: it tells A little. The pooled figure mixes the rules, so a high value can simply mean that the rule which flatters the pilot also helps at scale; the within-rule values are the fairer test.

Calibration: demand fitted to sales

With daily sales a regulator can fit the demand model itself: ln(si / s0) = ai / mu − pi / mu, by least squares with one intercept per seller. The fitted market gives competitive and monopoly prices, and the excess price index says where observed prices sit between them (0 competitive, 1 monopoly).

40 of 40 fits are usable. Fitted mu is off by 0.011 on average (mean error 0.006). The fitted competitive price is off by £0.02 on average. Ranked by the excess price index, held up by a threat has an AUC of 0.85; high prices has an AUC of 1.00.
