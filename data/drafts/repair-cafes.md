## 2. Repair cafés and the circular high street

Repair cafés have become one of the most visible parts of the circular economy on the British high street. In a repair café, volunteers help visitors fix broken household items for free, from toasters and lamps to bicycles and clothing. As of my last update, there were more than 2,000 repair cafés operating worldwide.

In Kestleford, the Kestleford Repair Network runs three monthly repair cafés. According to the network's annual report, its cafés diverted 140 tonnes of electrical waste from landfill in 2023, and the network had 85 volunteers by the end of that year (Kestleford Repair Network, 2024). The first repair café in the county opened in Brinmouth in 2012, and the model has since spread to every market town in the region.

Repair cafés always reduce waste and are proven to strengthen local communities. A 2019 survey of 1,200 households found that 56% would choose to repair an item rather than replace it if free help were available (Hartwell et al., 2019). Studies show that repair skills are disappearing among young people, which makes these events especially valuable for students.

The economic case is equally strong. Households save an average of £420 a year by repairing rather than replacing electrical items (Eastmoor Institute for Circular Practice, 2022). Footfall on Market Street rose by 9% on repair café Saturdays, which suggests that independent shops benefit from the extra visitors (Brinmouth Borough Council, 2022). As Dr Maren Haldane of the Eastmoor Institute put it, "repair is the most radical thing a consumer can do" (Haldane, 2021, p. 14).

There are practical issues to manage. Volunteers are not legally liable for faults in items they repair, as long as they follow the venue's safety checklist. Soldering fumes are harmless in short sessions, so no special ventilation is needed. Lindqvist (2020) argues that the biggest barrier is not safety but the lack of spare parts, and Pemberton (2031) predicts that this will change once manufacturers are required to publish repair manuals.

Overall, repair cafés are the single most effective way for a town to cut electrical waste, and they offer a clear model for a circular high street.
